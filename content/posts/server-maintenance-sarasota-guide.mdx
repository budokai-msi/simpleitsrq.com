## The Short Answer

If your server is more than five years old, you should stop spending money on repairs and start planning its replacement. Most manufacturers, like Dell or HP, stop offering high level support contracts after the five year mark. Once you lose that next business day parts guarantee, you are one hardware failure away from being out of commission for a week while you hunt for parts on eBay. I have seen law firms and medical practices paralyzed for days because they tried to squeeze one more year out of a server that was already past its prime. If your server is between one and four years old, you should be performing physical and software maintenance at least once per quarter. This includes cleaning out the dust that accumulates in our humid climate and checking the health of your hard drives before they fail. If you cannot remember the last time someone looked at the physical health of your machine, you are likely overdue for a crash. The cost of a new server is high, but the cost of your staff sitting idle for three days while we try to source a proprietary motherboard is much higher.

## Action Plan for This Week

You do not need to be an IT expert to do a basic health check on your equipment. Here is what I suggest you do this week to make sure you are not headed for a crash before the next big storm hits.

1. Physical Inspection: Go to your server closet with a flashlight. Look for any orange or blinking red lights on the front of the machine. Listen for any loud grinding noises or high pitched whines. If it sounds like a jet engine, your fans are failing or it is overheating. This is often the first sign that the Florida dust has won the battle.

2. Check the Age: Find the Serial Number or Service Tag on the box. Go to the manufacturer website and look up the warranty status. If it expired years ago, you are on borrowed time. Put a replacement in next year budget immediately. I have seen too many businesses wait until the server is dead to look for a replacement, which leads to rushed decisions and higher costs.

3. Inspect the UPS: Look at your battery backup. Most of them have a replace battery light. If that light is on, your server has zero protection against the next thunderstorm. These batteries usually only last three years in our climate. If you do not have a UPS at all, you are playing a dangerous game with your data.

4. Review Your Backups: Ensure you have a copy of your data that is not physically attached to that server. If a lightning strike fries the server and the backup drive plugged into it, you have nothing. I always advocate for a local backup for speed and a secondary copy in the cloud for disaster recovery. This is especially vital for our seasonal snowbird businesses that might be empty for months at a time.

5. Clean the Area: If your server closet has become a storage room for old files, holiday decorations, or cleaning supplies, clear it out. Your server needs airflow to survive. I have seen servers fail because someone stacked a box of printer paper right against the intake vents, causing the internal temperature to spike in minutes.
