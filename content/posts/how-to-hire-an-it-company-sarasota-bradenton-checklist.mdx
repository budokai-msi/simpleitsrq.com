I've been on both sides of this conversation. As the owner of a [local IT company](/services), I've answered these questions from careful prospects dozens of times. And I've inherited clients who asked none of them, then spent a year discovering why that was expensive. Here's the checklist I'd use if I were hiring someone to run my own company's IT, with notes on what each question actually tells you.

## The Twelve Questions

**1. Where are your technicians physically located?**
Not "where is your office", where do the people who will touch your machines live. A surprising number of "Sarasota" IT companies route tickets to Tampa, Miami, or overseas. Nothing wrong with remote work until you need boots in your office before lunch.

**2. Who answers the phone when something breaks at 8 AM?**
A named engineer beats a ticketing portal for emergencies. If the answer involves an "intake process," probe deeper. Ask what percentage of calls reach a human technician first try.

**3. What's your average ticket response time, and can you prove it?**
Any provider tracking this has the number. Vague answers ("we're very responsive") mean nobody measures it, which usually means it's bad. Our number last quarter was under 20 minutes for remote start; any honest shop can produce theirs instantly.

**4. Do you monitor our backups by restoring from them?**
This one catches more fake MSPs than anything else. Monitoring backup *jobs* means checking that a green checkmark appeared. Restoring from backups quarterly proves the data is real. The difference only matters the day you lose everything, and by then it's too late to switch providers.

**5. What happens to our passwords if we leave you?**
The wrong answer is anything vague. Passwords, documentation, and configurations belong to you and must be handed over complete, in a standard format, within days of your request. Some providers hold credentials hostage as de facto retention policy.

**6. Are you quoting per-user or per-device?**
If they haven't already made this clear, make them count everything. This single ambiguity explains most "surprise" invoice growth in year one.

**7. What security stack is included versus extra?**
Antivirus, email filtering, DNS protection, endpoint detection, password manager, awareness training. Get the list itemized. "Enterprise-grade security included" is marketing; a line-item list is a product.

**8. How do you handle after-hours emergencies, really?**
Get their actual after-hours rate, definition of hours, and typical response time. Then ask what fraction of their clients' emergency calls happen outside business hours (it's usually a third or more).

**9. Can we talk to two current clients similar to us in size and industry?**
References are table stakes; *similar* references are the real test. An MSP great with dental offices may fumble a law firm's document management, and vice versa.

**10. What does your onboarding look like, week by week?**
Good onboarding takes 2–6 weeks: discovery, documentation, patching backlog burn-down, backup verification. Bad onboarding is "we'll get you into the system next week." The answer tells you how they'll treat everything else.

**11. What tools do you use to monitor and manage endpoints, and do we license them or do you?**
You want named products (RMM, EDR, backup) and clarity on whether licenses transfer if you leave. Tool transparency separates operators from resellers.

**12. Is there anything about our environment that worries you?**
My favorite question. Honest providers will find something during discovery and tell you straight, unpatched servers, a domain controller past end-of-life, no tested restore. A provider who promises everything is fine either hasn't looked or isn't going to tell you.

## Red Flags Between the Questions

Watch how they sell, not just what they say. Fear-based pitches ("did you know 60% of small businesses close after ransomware?") target emotions because facts aren't on their side. Lock-in tactics, proprietary portals, contracts with 90-day renewal windows, refusal to document in your name, predict exactly how the relationship ends. And be wary of anyone who badmouths your current provider by name; professionals critique setups, not companies.

## Action Plan for This Week

1. Copy these twelve questions into an email and send it to every provider you're considering. Their speed and completeness in answering is itself diagnostic.
2. Score responses side by side. Vague answers score zero, not neutral, vagueness is information.
3. Check reviews filtered to business clients specifically, not residential computer repair.
4. Verify physical presence: a local address plus technicians who live here. Google Street View works; so does asking which day last month they were on-site near your office.
