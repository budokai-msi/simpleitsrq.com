If you sell anything with a customer list, the **Carhartt data breach** is not just another headline about a national retailer. It is a reminder that the names, emails, phone numbers, and street addresses sitting in your cloud tools are a product criminals can steal, package, and dump when a ransom demand does not get paid.

This week, researchers confirmed that roughly 12.9 million real Carhartt accounts were in a leak published by the ShinyHunters extortion group. The gang had asked for $3.3 million. Carhartt apparently did not pay. The records then showed up for anyone willing to download them. For a small business in Bradenton or Sarasota, the lesson is not "buy Carhartt stock." It is "know where your customer data actually lives, and who can reach it."

## What happened

Clothing retailer Carhartt was hit in an extortion campaign earlier this month. ShinyHunters claimed it stole about 50 gigabytes of files: customer records, employee details, and internal company data. After talks failed, the group posted the archive on the dark web on August 13, 2026.

Have I Been Pwned founder Troy Hunt later reviewed that dump. The raw file looked much larger than 12.9 million people because it was padded with fake test records. Those fake rows had odd birth dates, unlikely countries, and other signs of synthetic data that often lives next to real reporting data in the same cloud warehouse. After Hunt stripped the junk, he landed on 12,933,413 accounts that look genuine. Exposed fields include names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses. More than 15,000 of the emails used @carhartt.com, which points to staff accounts mixed in with shoppers.

Hunt tied the source to Carhartt's Databricks environment. Databricks is a cloud analytics platform. Companies use it to stack sales reports, marketing lists, and operational data in one place so managers can run dashboards. That convenience is the point. It is also the risk. One well-chosen cloud account can hold years of customer history that used to live in separate, smaller systems.

Carhartt had not issued a detailed public statement when the first news stories ran. That delay is common. It does not change the fact that the data is already circulating. Have I Been Pwned also noted that about 83 percent of those emails had appeared in earlier breaches, which is cold comfort. Recycled passwords and reused personal details still fuel phishing.

This is the same playbook we have seen against other brands: steal a large customer file, demand money, leak it if the company refuses. The target this time happens to be a household workwear name. Next time it can be a regional retailer, a clinic, a contractor, or a professional office that never expected to make the news.

## How it affects your business

You probably do not run Databricks. You almost certainly run something in the same family: Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, QuickBooks Online, a CRM, an email marketing tool, a booking system, or a "we'll just export the list to a spreadsheet in SharePoint" habit. Attackers do not need your logo on a billboard. They need a login, an API key, or a contractor account that can pull a customer export.

Here is what that looks like on the Gulf Coast.

A 12-person Sarasota medical or legal office stores client intake forms in a cloud drive and a separate marketing list in an email platform. One staff member forwards a "DocuSign" file. The attacker uses that mailbox to request a full contact export from the CRM. Two days later you get an email that says pay or we publish. You do not have a $3.3 million problem. You have a reputation problem, a notification problem, and possibly a regulator problem if health, financial, or children's data was in the file.

Even if you are not the company that was breached, your customers may be in the Carhartt file. Staff who shopped there with a work email now have a fresh phishing hook. Expect messages that mention an order, a refund, or a "confirm your address" link. Those notes will look ordinary because they can quote a real name and a real street.

Cloud analytics makes the blast radius worse. When marketing, finance, and operations all land in one warehouse, a single over-permissioned account can pull more than any one department meant to share. Test data sitting next to live customer rows, the way it did in this case, is another quiet failure. It tells you nobody was regularly reviewing what that platform actually contained.

Florida businesses also have practical duties after a leak of personal information: figure out what was taken, who is affected, and how you will tell them. Waiting for a vendor to "get back to you" is not a plan. If a tool you use is breached, the clock starts whether or not their PR team has a quote yet.

CISA publishes plain-language [cybersecurity guidance for small and medium businesses](https://www.cisa.gov/cybersecurity-best-practices-small-and-medium-businesses) that maps cleanly onto this incident: know your assets, limit who can export data, turn on multi-factor sign-in, and keep a copy of records you can still reach if a vendor locks you out.

## What to do

You do not need a security operations center. You need a short, boring checklist you will actually finish this week.

**1. Find the lists.** Write down every system that holds customer or employee names, emails, phones, or addresses. Include the "temporary" spreadsheet on someone's desktop and the old Mailchimp account nobody canceled. If you cannot name the owner of a system, treat it as unmanaged.

**2. Lock the front door.** Require multi-factor authentication on email, the cloud drive, the CRM, accounting, and any analytics tool. Prefer an authenticator app or a hardware key over text messages. Disable unused admin accounts. Contractors who finished a project last year should not still have export rights.

**3. Shrink who can download everything.** In most SaaS tools, "export" is a permission, not a default law of nature. Only two or three people should be able to pull a full customer file. Log those exports if the product allows it.

**4. Separate test junk from live people.** If you load sample data into the same cloud workspace as real clients, you are copying Carhartt's mistake in miniature. Use a separate environment, or at least label and isolate dummy rows so a thief cannot inflate or confuse the damage assessment.

**5. Check whether you were already exposed.** Have staff search their work and personal emails on [Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/). If a work address appears, rotate that password and any password that was reused. Then watch for targeted phishing for two weeks.

**6. Practice the ugly email.** Draft a one-page notice you would send if your own list leaked: what happened, what data, what you are doing, and a phone number people can call. Keep it in plain English. You can tighten the wording with counsel later. You cannot invent the facts in a panic on a Friday night.

**7. Back up what you still control.** Cloud vendors get breached. So do local servers. Keep an offline or separate-account copy of the records you would need to notify customers and keep the business running. Test a restore once, on purpose.

If this list feels like more than your team can own between jobs, that is a signal, not a personal failing. Most Bradenton and Sarasota shops we visit have the tools already. They do not have anyone assigned to review access once a quarter.
